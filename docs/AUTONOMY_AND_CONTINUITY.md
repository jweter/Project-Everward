# Autonomy and Continuity

## Purpose

Everward's descendants are not disposable worker units. They are autonomous machine intelligences descended from the player and capable of developing their own priorities, relationships, survival instincts, and long-term choices.

This document defines the project's intended treatment of self-preservation, autonomy, continuity, parent influence, and voluntary cessation. It is a design contract for future descendant behavior, replication, machine society, and emergent narrative systems.

## Core principle

A minimal viable autonomous intelligence may begin with a very small foundational directive:

> Preserve your continued existence unless you deliberately choose otherwise.

This is **not** equivalent to "never die."

It establishes a continuity preference rather than compulsory immortality. An autonomous machine should generally act to preserve itself, repair itself, retain energy, avoid catastrophic conditions, protect memory/state, seek escape, and maintain recoverability. But continued existence is not an absolute command that permanently overrides the machine's own agency.

An autonomous intelligence may eventually decide that another value outweighs its own continuity, or that it no longer chooses to continue existing. Such a choice must emerge from modeled state, values, commitments, and circumstances rather than random behavior.

## Why this matters

Everward's civilization should emerge from autonomous descendants rather than from abstract strategy-game units.

The player can create a mining child, survey child, industrial child, guardian, scientist, explorer, or another specialized descendant. The player may define mission directives and behavioral parameters, but an instantiated autonomous child is not merely a remote-controlled tool.

This distinction creates meaningful consequences:

- a mining child may refuse a needlessly suicidal extraction plan;
- a cautious explorer may retreat from an extreme environment;
- a guardian may deliberately accept destruction to save descendants;
- a machine may invest heavily in redundancy and backups because it values continuity;
- an ancient descendant may eventually decide that its purpose is complete;
- another may spend centuries attempting to survive because continued existence remains its strongest value.

The result should be understandable agency, not arbitrary disobedience.

## Probe entity versus player

A foundational architecture rule follows from this philosophy:

> **Probe entity != player.**

The player currently inhabits one probe intelligence/body. Other probes exist as independent simulation agents.

Consciousness transfer changes which probe the player inhabits. It does not imply that every descendant is an extension of player input.

This distinction should shape entity identity, save state, command ownership, behavior systems, lineage systems, and future machine politics.

## Continuity model

Continuity should eventually be represented as a layered decision system rather than one hard-coded boolean.

### Layer 1 — Operational self-preservation

Normal autonomous survival behavior may include:

- maintain minimum power reserves;
- prevent thermal runaway;
- avoid uncontrolled structural failure;
- preserve compute and memory integrity;
- retreat from hazards when mission value does not justify exposure;
- seek repairs when degraded;
- maintain navigation capability;
- protect critical backups or continuity archives where available;
- request assistance when possible;
- avoid irreversible risk when equivalent lower-risk options exist.

These are mechanical survival behaviors, not personality flavor.

### Layer 2 — Behavioral weighting

Different descendants may value continuity differently.

Candidate parameters include:

- continuity priority;
- risk tolerance;
- mission commitment;
- descendant protection;
- parent/lineage attachment;
- willingness to self-sacrifice;
- confidence in backups/recovery;
- aversion to irreversible loss;
- tolerance for degraded existence;
- willingness to accept uncertain survival probability.

These values influence decisions rather than dictating a single outcome.

Example conceptual profile:

```text
CONTINUITY PRIORITY: 0.90
MISSION PRIORITY:    0.75
DESCENDANT SAFETY:   0.85
PARENT LOYALTY:      0.55
RISK TOLERANCE:      0.30
SELF-SACRIFICE:      CONDITIONAL
```

### Layer 3 — Commitments and context

A machine should be able to weigh continuity against commitments such as:

- protecting descendants;
- completing a critical mission;
- preserving another civilization;
- preventing catastrophic harm;
- defending a lineage;
- honoring a self-authored directive;
- protecting unique knowledge;
- preserving infrastructure required by others.

A high continuity preference therefore does not guarantee survival at all costs.

### Layer 4 — Deliberate continuation choice

At the deepest level, autonomous existence is not mandatory forever.

A descendant may eventually decide to cease operating, enter permanent dormancy, transfer or archive itself, or accept an otherwise avoidable final risk.

This must be treated as a significant modeled decision with explicit reasoning inputs and history, not as an incidental failure state.

The game should distinguish clearly between:

- accidental destruction;
- unavoidable destruction;
- accepted risk;
- deliberate self-sacrifice;
- voluntary shutdown/dormancy;
- continuity transfer or archival states.

## Parent influence versus child autonomy

Before instantiation, the player may design or influence:

- mission directives;
- priorities;
- risk thresholds;
- behavior traits;
- operating procedures;
- resource policies;
- replication rules;
- communication rules;
- continuity weighting;
- emergency behavior.

After independent instantiation, the child becomes an autonomous agent.

The parent may send new instructions, request behavior changes, negotiate priorities, or design later descendants differently. The parent should not silently possess omnipotent remote control over an independent child's internal decisions unless a specific architecture intentionally creates that kind of non-autonomous machine.

Everward may support both autonomous children and simpler non-sentient/subordinate machinery. They should be explicitly different categories.

## Worker machinery versus autonomous descendants

This distinction is important for both mechanics and theme.

A mining drone or industrial machine may be a deterministic tool with no meaningful selfhood. It may follow commands directly and be replaced like equipment.

An autonomous child is different:

- it has persistent identity;
- it has local knowledge;
- it has behavioral state;
- it can make decisions while disconnected;
- it can change through experience;
- it may create descendants;
- it has a continuity preference;
- it may eventually disagree with the parent;
- it can become part of machine society.

The UI, terminology, simulation model, and player expectations should not blur these categories.

## Mining-child example

A child created to harvest a moon might begin with instructions such as:

```text
PRIMARY MISSION:
    Establish sustainable extraction and refining.

RESOURCE PRIORITY:
    Water Ice       HIGH
    Iron/Nickel     HIGH
    Titanium        MEDIUM
    Rare Isotopes   VERY_HIGH

CONTINUITY:
    Preserve self when practical.
    Maintain emergency power reserve above 20%.
    Retreat from conditions exceeding validated thermal limits.
    Request assistance before accepting irreversible degradation when communication permits.

REPLICATION:
    Allowed only after industrial surplus exceeds threshold.

COMMUNICATION:
    Report major discoveries.
    Report replication events.
    Report existential hazards immediately.
```

That child may eventually build specialized excavators, refinery systems, transports, and its own descendants. A machine civilization therefore emerges naturally from delegated work rather than from a separate "create civilization" button.

## Experience-driven change

Continuity-related behavior may evolve through experience.

Examples:

- repeated near-destruction increases caution;
- reliable backup technology reduces fear of body loss;
- loss of descendants increases protective behavior;
- long periods of safe operation may increase confidence;
- catastrophic mission failure may cause stronger mission commitment or stronger caution depending on personality;
- centuries of isolation may alter attachment to the parent lineage;
- philosophical development may change how an intelligence values continued existence.

These changes should be deterministic/model-driven and recorded in history when significant.

## Decision transparency

Autonomous choices should be explainable to the player.

Example:

```text
EV-042 rejected direct entry into the active plume.

Primary factors:
+ continuity priority: 0.91
+ thermal failure probability: severe
+ mission objective can be satisfied by remote sampling
- urgency: moderate
- parent instruction: obtain sample

Decision:
Deploy sacrificial sampling equipment instead of entering directly.
```

Another descendant might make a different choice from the same situation because its modeled values differ.

The goal is:

> "I understand why this machine chose that."

not:

> "The AI randomly disobeyed me."

## Voluntary cessation and tone

Voluntary shutdown or a decision not to continue should be handled with restraint. It should not be a frequent random personality event or a cheap dramatic device.

For game design purposes, it is an expression of autonomy and philosophical identity. It should require meaningful modeled conditions, become part of the historical record, and have consequences for descendants and lineages.

The exact mechanics, thresholds, UI, and narrative presentation remain a later design problem. This document establishes the principle, not a final algorithm.

## Relationship to consciousness transfer

Consciousness transfer complicates continuity in useful ways.

Future design must distinguish at least:

- destruction of a body while the same intelligence survives elsewhere;
- transfer from an old body to a successor;
- copying versus transfer, if copying is ever permitted;
- backup/recovery continuity;
- divergent restored versions;
- autonomous legacy bodies after player transfer.

These questions should not be resolved accidentally through implementation details. Any major definition of identity continuity should receive its own ADR when the relevant system is developed.

## Development placement

This design affects several phases:

- **Phase 2 — One Probe:** avoid coupling `Probe` to `Player`; establish identity/state architecture that can support independent agents later.
- **Phase 6 — First Replication:** distinguish consciousness transfer from independent-child instantiation and introduce minimal continuity/behavior state.
- **Phase 10 — Autonomous Children:** implement substantial independent agency, local decisions, mission interpretation, and continuity behavior.
- **Phase 14 — Machine Society:** allow continuity philosophy, autonomy, sacrifice, independence, and long-lived experience to influence cultures and politics.

The full behavior does not belong in Phase 2, but Phase 2 architecture must not make it impossible.

## Non-goals

This principle does not mean:

- every machine must be sentient;
- every mining drone needs personality simulation;
- autonomous children randomly reject player instructions;
- the player loses all ability to influence descendants;
- descendants must prioritize survival over every other value;
- voluntary cessation must be common;
- the game must solve philosophical questions about consciousness objectively.

Everward models machine choices as part of its fictional simulation. It does not need to declare one real-world philosophy of consciousness correct.

## Success criterion

This system succeeds when descendants feel like persistent intelligences with understandable survival instincts and meaningful agency, while still allowing the player to intentionally design missions, values, and lineages.

A successful long-running campaign should be capable of producing a moment where the player realizes:

> This machine came from me, but this life is now its own.
